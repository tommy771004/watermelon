Inside the experiment

One field, many pieces. The melon is a signed distance field: an ellipsoid 32 cm long with a softly flattened belly. Skin, pith and flesh are not textures but depths below that surface, so a cut face shows rind rings exactly where they grew. Each piece is the same field clipped by the half-spaces of the cuts that made it, its fresh edges rounded with a smooth max.

The knife. Your stroke is read on top of what it crosses and becomes a vertical blade plane. Each piece in its path is fitted to its best rotation, the plane is carried back into rest space and the piece is split. New render skins (surface nets), hex lattices and embeddings are built a few milliseconds per frame while the blade presses a groove; the halves swap in the moment the edge breaks through, inheriting the motion of the flesh they came from.

The jelly. Every lattice cell is shape-matched to its best rotation (XPBD, 60 Hz, six substeps) with strain stiffening and tetrahedral volume constraints; the rind is firmer than the flesh. Damping acts on deformation relative to each piece's rigid motion, never on its fall.

The light. Front faces refract the studio behind them by the thickness of jelly along the view ray, absorbing per layer (Beer–Lambert) and scattering milky light in the pith. Seeds sit a film beneath each cut, seen through the jelly. Shadows are tinted by what the light crossed first.
